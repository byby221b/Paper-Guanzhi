# Classical AI (1956–1979) — The Golden Age of Symbolic AI

> From the Dartmouth Conference to expert systems, AI's first generation established the fundamental frameworks for search, planning, reasoning, and knowledge representation.

## Learning & Pattern Recognition

| Year | Paper | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|-------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1957 | **The Perceptron: A Probabilistic Model for Information Storage and Organization in the Brain** | Frank Rosenblatt | Psychological Review, Vol. 65, pp. 386–408 | The first trainable artificial neural network — proved the perceptron convergence theorem, launching the first wave of neural network research |
| 1959 | **Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers** | Arthur Samuel | IBM Journal of Research and Development, Vol. 3, No. 3, pp. 210–229 | The first self-learning game-playing program — coined the term "machine learning" and implemented self-play and evaluation function learning |

## Search & Problem Solving

| Year | Paper | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|-------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1959 | **Pandemonium: A Paradigm for Learning** | Oliver Selfridge | Proc. Symposium on Mechanisation of Thought Processes, pp. 511–529 | Proposed the "Pandemonium" model — a pioneer of multi-layer competitive feature detection, foreshadowing hierarchical feature learning in deep networks |
| 1960 | **Report on a General Problem-Solving Program (GPS)** | Allen Newell, J.C. Shaw, Herbert Simon | Proc. International Conference on Information Processing, pp. 256–264 | The General Problem Solver — first formalized means-ends analysis, becoming the starting point for AI planning research |
| 1968 | **A Formal Basis for the Heuristic Determination of Minimum Cost Paths** | Peter Hart, Nils Nilsson, Bertram Raphael | IEEE Trans. Systems Science and Cybernetics, Vol. 4, No. 2, pp. 100–107 | The A\* search algorithm — proved optimal and complete, still the most widely used search algorithm in path planning, game AI, and natural language parsing |

## Logical Reasoning

| Year | Paper | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|-------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1965 | **A Machine-Oriented Logic Based on the Resolution Principle** | John Alan Robinson | Journal of the ACM, Vol. 12, No. 1, pp. 23–41 | The Resolution Principle — unified automated theorem proving in first-order logic into a single inference rule, becoming the theoretical foundation for Prolog and logic programming |
| 1963 | **Situations, Actions, and Causal Laws** | John McCarthy | Stanford AI Project Memo 2 | The Situation Calculus — the first formal framework for reasoning about actions in AI, directly inspiring STRIPS and subsequent planning research |

## Planning

| Year | Paper | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|-------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1971 | **STRIPS: A New Approach to the Application of Theorem Proving to Problem Solving** | Richard Fikes, Nils Nilsson | Artificial Intelligence, Vol. 2, Nos. 3–4, pp. 189–208 | The STRIPS planning language — defined the "state-action-effect" planning representation; a direct ancestor of PDDL and all modern planning languages |

## Natural Language & Semantics

| Year | Paper / Book | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|-------------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1966 | **ELIZA — A Computer Program for the Study of Natural Language Communication between Man and Machine** | Joseph Weizenbaum | Communications of the ACM, Vol. 9, No. 1, pp. 36–45 | The first dialogue system — achieved convincingly human-like conversation through simple pattern matching, provoking deep reflections on the nature of human-computer interaction |
| 1972 | **Understanding Natural Language** (SHRDLU) | Terry Winograd | New York: Academic Press | Achieved natural language understanding and dialogue in a blocks world — demonstrated the integration of syntax, semantics, and reasoning. Though limited to a microworld, it inspired the entire NLP field |
| 1966 | **Semantic Memory** | M. Ross Quillian | Ph.D. dissertation, Carnegie Institute of Technology | The foundational work on semantic networks — modeled human conceptual memory as an associative network, influencing the development of knowledge graphs and ontologies |

## Knowledge Representation & Expert Systems

| Year | Paper / Book | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|-------------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1969 | **Perceptrons** | Marvin Minsky, Seymour Papert | Cambridge, MA: MIT Press | Rigorously proved the limitations of single-layer perceptrons (e.g., inability to learn XOR) — directly caused neural network research to enter a decade-long "winter," but also pushed the field toward multi-layer networks |
| 1974 | **A Framework for Representing Knowledge** | Marvin Minsky | MIT AI Lab Memo 306 | Frame theory — proposed structured "frames" for representing commonsense knowledge, profoundly influencing object-oriented programming and knowledge engineering |
| 1976 | **Computer Science as Empirical Inquiry: Symbols and Search** | Allen Newell, Herbert Simon | Communications of the ACM, Vol. 19, No. 3, pp. 113–126 | Turing Award Lecture — proposed the Physical Symbol System Hypothesis, arguing that symbol manipulation is both necessary and sufficient for intelligent behavior, defining the philosophical stance of classical AI |
| 1977 | **The Art of Artificial Intelligence: Themes and Case Studies of Knowledge Engineering** | Edward Feigenbaum | Proc. IJCAI-5, pp. 1014–1029 | The Knowledge Engineering manifesto — drawing on DENDRAL and MYCIN experience, argued that "knowledge is power," launching the expert systems era |
| 1977 | **Scripts, Plans, Goals, and Understanding** | Roger Schank, Robert Abelson | Hillsdale, NJ: Lawrence Erlbaum Associates | Script theory — proposed "scripts" for representing stereotypical event sequences, pioneering work in narrative understanding and commonsense reasoning |

## Computer Vision

| Year | Book | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1982 | **Vision** | David Marr | San Francisco: W.H. Freeman | Computational vision theory — proposed the three-level framework from raw images to 2.5-D to 3-D representations. Though specific proposals are now dated, the "computational theory – algorithm – implementation" layered methodology remains a cornerstone of vision research[^1] |

[^1]: Published one year after Marr's death. Though technically outside the 1979 cutoff, it intellectually belongs to the classical AI era as its capstone work.

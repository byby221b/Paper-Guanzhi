# Pre-AI (1936–1956) — The Intellectual Origins of AI

> Before "artificial intelligence" had a name, these papers laid its mathematical, logical, and engineering foundations.

## Mathematical Logic & Theory of Computation

| Year | Paper | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|-------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1936 | **On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem** | Alan Turing | Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society, Series 2, Vol. 42, pp. 230–265 | Introduced the Turing Machine, defining the mathematical boundaries of computability and laying the theoretical foundation for all of computer science |
| 1943 | **A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity** | Warren McCulloch, Walter Pitts | Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics, Vol. 5, pp. 115–133 | The first mathematical model of an artificial neuron — proved that networks of simple logical neurons can compute any computable function |

## Information Theory & Game Playing

| Year | Paper | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|-------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1948 | **A Mathematical Theory of Communication** | Claude Shannon | Bell System Technical Journal, Vol. 27, pp. 379–423, 623–656 | Founded information theory — defined "bit," "entropy," and "channel capacity," providing the measurement framework for all digital communication and, later, machine learning |
| 1950 | **Programming a Computer for Playing Chess** | Claude Shannon | Philosophical Magazine, Series 7, Vol. 41, No. 314 | The first systematic treatment of computer game playing — proposed Type A (brute-force) and Type B (selective) search strategies, shaping half a century of game AI research |

## Thought Experiments on Machine Intelligence

| Year | Paper | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|-------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1948 | **Intelligent Machinery** | Alan Turing | National Physical Laboratory Report (1948); reprinted in Machine Intelligence 5 (1969) | Turing's first systematic essay on machine intelligence — explored how "unorganized machines" could be trained to acquire intelligence, anticipating neural networks and genetic algorithms |
| 1950 | **Computing Machinery and Intelligence** | Alan Turing | Mind, Vol. LIX, No. 236, pp. 433–460 | Proposed the "Turing Test" — the first serious argument, from both philosophical and engineering perspectives, for the possibility that machines can think. Became AI's founding manifesto |

## Learning & Adaptation

| Year | Paper / Book | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|-------------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1949 | **The Organization of Behavior: A Neuropsychological Theory** | Donald Hebb | New York: John Wiley | Proposed the Hebb learning rule ("neurons that fire together wire together"), providing the physiological basis for neural network learning theory |
| 1854 | **An Investigation of the Laws of Thought** | George Boole | Dover Publications | Established Boolean algebra — reduced logical reasoning to algebraic manipulation, foundational for digital circuit design and symbolic AI reasoning[^1] |

[^1]: Though published long before the AI era, its influence runs directly through to modern SAT solvers and knowledge representation.

## The Birth of AI

| Year | Paper | Authors | Venue | Significance |
|------|-------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1955 | **A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence** | John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, Claude Shannon | Unpublished; reprinted in AI Magazine, Vol. 27, No. 4, 2006 | The birth of the term "Artificial Intelligence" — this proposal defined AI as an independent discipline and convened the first AI workshop (the 1956 Dartmouth Conference) |
| 1956 | **The Logic Theory Machine: A Complex Information Processing System** | Allen Newell, Herbert Simon | IRE Transactions on Information Theory, Vol. IT-2, pp. 61–79 | The first AI program in history — automatically proved 38 theorems from *Principia Mathematica*, pioneering the heuristic search paradigm |
| 1956 | **The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two** | George Miller | Psychological Review, Vol. 63, pp. 81–97 | A milestone in cognitive psychology — revealed the capacity limits of human short-term memory, profoundly influencing AI knowledge representation and human-computer interaction design |
